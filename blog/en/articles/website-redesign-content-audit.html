Website Content Audit Before a Redesign: Keep What Works

A redesign changes more than the visual layer. Audit the current site before design and development begin so useful content, search visibility, customer journeys and important URLs are not lost in the migration.

RocketPen Digital Studio Editorial TeamUpdated 2026-10-22Practical guide

Why the audit belongs before design

Redesign projects often begin with screenshots, layouts and feature requests. But the current website already contains information about what customers need, which pages attract relevant visitors and where the experience breaks down. If the team replaces URLs or removes content before reviewing that evidence, it may discard resources that support discovery, trust or enquiries.

A content audit is not a vote to preserve every old page. It is a structured way to decide what should stay, change, combine or go, and to record how those decisions will be implemented in the new site.

1. Crawl and inventory the existing site

Build a URL inventory using a crawler, sitemap, analytics exports and Search Console. These sources serve different purposes: a crawler reveals links and response codes, a sitemap lists URLs the site declares important, analytics shows recorded visits and actions, and Search Console provides search visibility data. No single source is a complete list of every URL that matters.

For each URL, capture the current title, meta description, heading, status code, canonical URL, indexability, page type, language, internal links and destination of key calls to action. Record redirects and URLs found through backlinks or external campaigns where that information is available. Keep the original export so the team can compare it with the launch version.

2. Add evidence about value and purpose

Combine search impressions and clicks with relevant analytics events, qualified enquiries, lead quality and customer feedback. Note backlinks or referral traffic where available. Interpret low-volume pages in context: a policy page, niche service page or detailed answer may be important even if it attracts fewer visits than the homepage.

Write one sentence describing the job each page performs and the audience it serves. If that sentence is difficult to write, the page may be unclear, redundant or poorly structured. If several pages serve the same job, compare their actual content before deciding to consolidate them.

3. Decide what to keep, improve, merge or retire

  • Keep: accurate content that serves a distinct need and performs its job. Preserve the URL where feasible.
  • Improve: pages with a continuing purpose but outdated information, weak structure, poor accessibility or unclear next steps.
  • Merge: overlapping pages where a single, coherent resource can satisfy the combined intent better. Move valuable unique details into the destination.
  • Retire: obsolete or unnecessary pages with no suitable replacement. Check links, legal or operational requirements and historical value before removal.

Document the reason, owner, target URL and evidence for each decision. Avoid deleting pages solely because they have low traffic or because a new design has fewer content slots. The redesigned information architecture should follow user needs rather than force all content into a predetermined template.

4. Design the new information architecture

Group pages around the way visitors understand your services, products and information. Make navigation labels descriptive, keep important tasks easy to reach and provide clear relationships between overview pages and detailed resources. Check mobile navigation and keyboard operation as part of the design, not as a late polish step.

For each page type, define the content needed to make a decision: audience, problem, service scope, process, limitations, evidence, FAQs and next action where relevant. Do not copy every section from the old site by default, but do not remove useful information merely because it is difficult to fit into a visual mockup.

5. Map every old URL to a deliberate outcome

Create a redirect map before launch. For each old URL, identify the corresponding new URL if a genuinely equivalent page exists. A permanent redirect is appropriate when content has moved or a suitable replacement has been consolidated. Avoid sending unrelated pages to the homepage or a generic page just to make every old URL return a success response.

Update internal links so they point directly to final destinations instead of passing through redirects. Check for chains, loops, broken targets and incorrect language or trailing-slash variants. Review canonical tags, hreflang relationships where used, robots directives and XML sitemaps against the final architecture. Include image, PDF and campaign URLs when they are part of the migration scope.

6. Preserve measurement and conversion paths

Record current analytics events and conversion definitions before replacing templates or forms. Decide which events need to remain, which are obsolete and what new events the redesign requires. Test enquiry forms, booking flows, downloads, phone links and other important actions in the staging environment, including success and error states.

Make sure consent settings and privacy requirements are respected. A change in analytics implementation can create an apparent traffic or conversion drop even when user behaviour has not changed. Document tracking changes so post-launch reporting can distinguish measurement changes from real performance changes.

7. Run a pre-launch and post-launch QA pass

Before launch

  • Compare the staging crawl with the approved URL inventory.
  • Confirm page titles, descriptions, headings, canonical tags and indexability.
  • Test internal links, redirects, forms, downloads and important user journeys.
  • Check mobile layout, keyboard access, contrast and meaningful image alternatives.
  • Verify structured data and ensure the staging environment will not accidentally block the production site after launch.

After launch

  • Crawl the live site and sample important old-to-new URL mappings.
  • Check server responses, redirect chains, canonical URLs and sitemap accessibility.
  • Inspect Search Console indexing and crawl reports as data becomes available.
  • Verify analytics events and conversion records with real test journeys.
  • Monitor relevant traffic, enquiries and error logs against the baseline.

Automated checks are useful, but manually test the highest-value customer journeys. A successful deployment does not guarantee every link, form or redirect works as intended.

8. Make the launch plan realistic

  1. Discovery: inventory URLs, collect performance data and identify technical constraints.
  2. Decisions: approve keep/improve/merge/retire outcomes and the new information architecture.
  3. Build: prepare content, redirect mappings, tracking requirements and QA cases alongside design and development.
  4. Release: test redirects, indexability, forms and analytics before and immediately after launch.
  5. Review: compare post-launch signals with the baseline, fix issues and document lessons.

Assign a named owner to content, redirects, analytics and technical QA. The launch checklist should identify who verifies each item and what evidence counts as completion.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should we change every URL during a redesign?

No. Keep URLs where practical, especially when they already serve a clear purpose. If a URL must change, map it to a relevant destination and test the permanent redirect.

Can we remove pages with little traffic?

Only after reviewing purpose, search visibility, backlinks, customer journeys and operational requirements. Low traffic alone is not enough to establish that a page has no value.

When should SEO be involved?

During discovery and information architecture, before templates and URLs are finalised. Early review makes it easier to protect useful pages and build redirects into the delivery plan.

How should we measure the redesign?

Compare relevant search visibility, qualified visits, important conversions, technical errors and user feedback against a documented baseline. Account for tracking changes and external factors before attributing results to the redesign.
