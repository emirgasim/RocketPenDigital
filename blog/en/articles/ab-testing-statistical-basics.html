A/B Testing Basics: Hypotheses, Sample Size and Reliable Decisions

A practical A/B test starts with a customer problem, a measurable hypothesis and enough evidence to make a decision—not simply two page variants and a dashboard.

RocketPen Digital Studio Editorial TeamPractical guide

What A/B testing can—and cannot—tell you

An A/B test randomly assigns eligible visitors to two experiences and compares a predefined outcome. It is useful when a meaningful product or marketing decision can be isolated and enough traffic is available. It does not automatically explain why a variant won, prove that the result will persist, or compensate for poor instrumentation.

Use experimentation when the result could change a real decision: which form layout to ship, whether a pricing explanation improves qualified enquiries, or which onboarding step helps users finish setup. If the page has very little traffic, the offer is unclear, or tracking is unreliable, fix those foundations before testing small visual differences.

Write a falsifiable hypothesis

Describe the observed problem, the proposed change, the expected behaviour and the primary metric. For example: “Visitors abandon the quote form because the required information is unclear. Adding an example beside the company-size field should increase valid form completions without reducing lead quality.” This can be challenged by data; “the new design will look better” cannot.

  • Problem: a specific friction point supported by analytics, support conversations or usability research.
  • Change: one interpretable intervention, such as clarifying field requirements.
  • Primary outcome: the metric that decides the test.
  • Guardrails: measures that must not deteriorate, such as qualified-lead rate, error rate or page performance.

Choose one primary metric before launch. Looking at many metrics and reporting only the most favourable result increases the chance of a misleading conclusion.

Estimate sample size before launch

Sample size depends on the baseline conversion rate, the smallest effect worth detecting, the acceptable false-positive rate and the desired statistical power. A change from 2.0% to 2.1% requires far more observations than a change from 2.0% to 3.0%. Use a reputable sample-size calculator or statistical tool, and document the assumptions.

Do not stop the test the moment a dashboard shows a favourable result. Repeatedly checking significance and ending early when it looks good inflates false positives. Set the duration and stopping rule in advance, and account for weekly patterns, campaign changes and the time needed for conversions to mature.

If the required sample is unrealistic, choose a larger, decision-relevant effect, gather qualitative evidence, test a higher-traffic step or use a different evaluation method. A small sample does not become conclusive because the percentage lift looks impressive.

Protect the experiment from avoidable bias

  • Randomise eligible visitors consistently and keep each visitor in the same variant.
  • Run variants concurrently so seasonality and campaign mix affect them similarly.
  • Check that assignment, exposure and conversion events are recorded correctly.
  • Exclude internal traffic and known bots using a documented rule applied to both groups.
  • Avoid overlapping tests that change the same page or audience unless the design explicitly accounts for interactions.
  • Record outages, pricing changes and campaign launches that could affect interpretation.

Before analysing results, verify sample-ratio balance: if the allocation was intended to be even but the observed split is unexpectedly uneven, investigate the experiment implementation before trusting the outcome.

Read the result in business context

Report the conversion rates, absolute difference, relative difference, uncertainty interval, sample size and test dates. An increase from 4.0% to 4.4% is a 0.4 percentage-point increase and a 10% relative increase; those descriptions are not interchangeable. Translate the effect into a realistic business scenario only when traffic, lead quality and downstream value support the calculation.

A statistically detectable improvement may still be too small to justify engineering and maintenance costs. Conversely, a promising but inconclusive result may justify another test or qualitative investigation rather than an automatic rejection. Record the decision, owner and follow-up measurement.

A repeatable test workflow

  1. Diagnose: combine funnel data with session research, customer feedback and usability checks.
  2. Prioritise: rank ideas by expected impact, confidence, effort and strategic relevance.
  3. Specify: define eligibility, hypothesis, primary metric, guardrails, sample plan and stopping rule.
  4. QA: test both variants on supported devices, confirm events and check assignment.
  5. Run: avoid unplanned changes and document unusual events.
  6. Analyse: follow the plan, inspect data quality and report uncertainty honestly.
  7. Decide: ship, iterate, repeat or stop—and verify the result after release.

Frequently asked questions

How long should an A/B test run?

Long enough to reach the planned sample and cover relevant behaviour cycles, often including full weeks. The required duration depends on traffic, conversion frequency and the decision being tested; a fixed number of days is not universally valid.

Is a 95% confidence level enough to ship a change?

Not by itself. Check the design, data quality, effect size, uncertainty, guardrail metrics and cost of being wrong. Statistical evidence is one input to a business decision, not a substitute for judgement.

What if my website has low traffic?

Prioritise major friction points, conduct moderated usability tests, analyse support questions or test changes with larger expected effects. Avoid presenting an underpowered test as proof that two options perform equally.
