Content Strategy: Decide What to Publish, Maintain and Retire

A workable content strategy connects audience needs with business goals, editorial capacity and evidence. It helps a team choose which pages deserve investment—and which should be improved, combined or retired.

RocketPen Digital Studio Editorial TeamUpdated 2026-10-22Practical guide

What a content strategy should decide

A content strategy is more than a list of topics or a publishing calendar. It defines the audiences a business intends to help, the questions it can answer credibly, the role each content type plays and the resources available to maintain that work. It should also explain what the team will not create.

Without those decisions, teams often publish overlapping articles, let important service information become stale and judge progress by output volume. A smaller portfolio with clear purpose, evidence and ownership can serve customers better than a large archive no one has time to maintain.

1. Start with audience tasks and business outcomes

Describe the people you serve in terms of their goals and constraints, not only demographics. What are they trying to understand, compare, decide or do? What makes the decision difficult? Which questions repeatedly appear in sales calls, support tickets, onboarding sessions or customer interviews?

Connect those needs to a business outcome. Content may help a visitor understand a service, assess whether it fits, prepare for implementation, solve a recurring problem or choose the next step. Different goals call for different formats. An educational guide, a service page, a comparison and a troubleshooting article should not all make the same sales pitch.

Record the intended audience, their task, the evidence available and the next useful action for each proposed asset. If the team cannot state what distinct need the page serves, do not add it to the plan yet.

2. Audit the existing library before commissioning new work

Inventory the current pages and capture their titles, URLs, purpose, primary audience, owner, last review date, indexability, internal links and available performance measures. Where reliable data exists, include search impressions and clicks, qualified conversions, referral traffic and relevant engagement. Mark missing data as unknown rather than assuming a page has no value.

Read the actual page content. Similar titles can answer different questions, while differently titled pages may repeat the same advice. Compare the intended reader, main question, practical answer, evidence and next action. Note thin template copy, unsupported claims, stale screenshots, outdated instructions and pages that send readers in circles.

Assign each URL a provisional decision:

  • Keep: it answers a distinct need accurately and has a clear role.
  • Improve: the need is valid, but the page lacks depth, evidence, usability or current information.
  • Merge: it substantially overlaps another page and the surviving destination can satisfy both intents.
  • Retire or investigate: it is obsolete, duplicative or unsupported by a continuing user need. Check links, business obligations and available performance evidence before removal.

Do not decide based on word count or traffic alone. A page with few visits may still answer an important customer question, support sales or attract useful links. A high-traffic page may still be misleading or poorly aligned with the business.

3. Prioritise topics with a transparent method

Use a simple scoring rubric to make trade-offs visible. Rate audience importance, relevance to the business, confidence in the evidence, distinctiveness of the proposed answer, maintenance effort and risk of overlap. Scores help structure a conversation; they are not objective truth and should not be presented as a prediction of rankings.

Search data can reveal demand and language, but combine it with interviews, support questions, sales objections and expertise the organisation can substantiate. If search-volume or traffic data is unavailable, say so. Do not label a topic popular or claim that a page has no audience without evidence.

Prioritise content that fills a real gap, improves an important decision or fixes a material accuracy problem. Avoid producing a separate URL for every keyword variant when one clear page can answer the related queries naturally.

4. Write briefs that protect originality

A useful brief specifies the reader, question, intended outcome, key points that must be covered, evidence or subject-matter review required, and the page's distinctive contribution. It also identifies what existing pages should link to it and what it should link to next.

Require examples that fit the actual subject. A guide to content governance should explain approval roles, version control, accessibility, legal review where relevant and what happens when an owner leaves. A guide to editorial calendars should explain capacity, dependencies, distribution and update triggers. Generic instructions to “research, implement and measure” do not substitute for topic-specific help.

Writers should distinguish facts, experience and recommendations. Verify technical claims, link to primary documentation when appropriate and avoid invented case studies, statistics or guaranteed outcomes. Use headings to help readers scan, not to repeat the same keyword. Remove filler even when the resulting article is shorter.

5. Build a sustainable editorial operating model

Assign an owner for every important asset and clarify who writes, reviews, approves, publishes and updates it. Agree on editorial standards for voice, factual accuracy, accessibility, image rights, privacy, citations and internal links. High-risk or specialist content may require subject-matter or legal review before publication.

Plan around real capacity. Estimate research, drafting, review, design, publishing and maintenance time—not just writing time. Leave room for updates and distribution. A calendar that assumes every week can support multiple new articles will fail if the team has no time to validate claims or refresh existing pages.

Set review triggers as well as dates. A page may need attention when a product changes, an official specification is updated, a service is discontinued, a process breaks or customer questions shift. The review date should prompt an assessment, not an automatic rewrite of still-accurate material.

6. Connect pages into a useful journey

Internal links should help a reader take the next logical step. An introductory article might link to a detailed implementation guide; that guide may point to a relevant service page, checklist or comparison. Use descriptive anchor text and link only when the destination genuinely adds value.

Check for orphan pages, repetitive “related articles” blocks and links that lead to retired URLs. When merging pages, update internal links to point directly to the chosen destination. If a URL is retired, use a permanent redirect only when a close, relevant replacement exists; do not redirect unrelated content to the homepage merely to avoid a not-found response.

7. Measure usefulness, then change the portfolio

Choose measures that match the job of the content. Search impressions and clicks can indicate discoverability; qualified visits and enquiries may matter for service pages; successful task completion or fewer repeat support questions may matter for help content. No single metric explains the full value of every asset.

Define the measurement window and baseline, note tracking limitations and consider seasonality or other changes before attributing a result to a rewrite. Use customer feedback and subject-matter review alongside analytics. If the data is incomplete, record that limitation rather than inventing certainty.

Review the portfolio periodically. Refresh pages that remain useful but are outdated, combine genuinely overlapping content, add missing answers where evidence supports the need and retire pages that no longer serve a clear purpose. Document the rationale and any redirect mapping so future editors understand the decision.

A practical 30-day starting plan

  • Week 1: agree on audience tasks, business outcomes, editorial constraints and decision owners.
  • Week 2: inventory the existing library, read representative pages and group overlapping intents.
  • Week 3: prioritise a small batch, write topic-specific briefs and improve the most consequential pages.
  • Week 4: update internal links and relevant redirects, validate technical details, publish changes and document the measurement baseline.

For a large archive, work in reviewable batches. Preserve the original URL inventory, keep a change log and validate each batch before starting the next. This makes mistakes easier to locate and reverse.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many articles should a content strategy include?

There is no universal target. The right size depends on the audience's distinct needs, the evidence and expertise available, and the team's capacity to keep content accurate. Publish to fill meaningful gaps, not to hit an arbitrary quota.

Should low-traffic pages be deleted?

Not automatically. Review the page's purpose, accuracy, links, conversions, role in the customer journey and available data. If it overlaps another page, consolidation may help; if its purpose remains important, improve it or investigate why it is hard to find.

When should two articles be merged?

Consider merging when they serve substantially the same audience and search intent and one destination can provide a better, complete answer. Preserve useful unique details, choose a relevant destination and map the old URL with a permanent redirect.

How often should content be reviewed?

Use risk and change rate to set the cadence. Technical instructions, product details and regulated topics may need more frequent review than evergreen explanatory material. Also review content when a meaningful change makes it inaccurate.

Is keyword volume enough to prioritise a topic?

No. Search demand is one input. Audience relevance, business value, the ability to provide a distinctive answer, competition, evidence and maintenance cost all matter. Where volume data is missing, do not make popularity claims.
